Water around a water heater is one of the most common calls we get, and the first question is always the same: where is it actually coming from? Water travels along pipes and collects wherever the floor dips, so the puddle is often nowhere near the source. It might be the temperature and pressure relief valve or its discharge line, the drain valve near the bottom of the tank, the inlet and outlet fittings on top, condensation on a cold tank, or a nearby pipe that has nothing to do with the heater. It might also be the tank itself, and that answer changes everything. In Franklin, TN, we start every leak call by finding the true source.

We treat every leak like a small investigation. The technician dries everything off so fresh water shows where it starts, then checks the relief valve and its discharge line, the drain valve at the base, and every connection on top. On gas models we also inspect the burner compartment, because water there can be normal startup condensation or a sign of a tank that has failed. A relief valve that keeps releasing water means we also test pressure: static pressure above roughly 80 psi, or a missing expansion tank on a closed system, will keep a new valve weeping. The fix follows the findings.

Most leaks are handled the same day we find them. If the tank itself is leaking, there is no safe patch, and you will hear that from us directly along with your replacement options. We also help prevent the next leak from doing damage: a drain pan piped to a proper drain, a water sensor or automatic shutoff valve, and a clear label on the shutoff. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Rust-tinted hot water and signs of seepage near the base
  • Mineral buildup or rust around the hot and cold connections
  • The water meter moving when nothing in the house is running
  • Water inside the burner compartment of a gas heater
  • Water dripping from the relief valve or out of the end of its discharge pipe
  • Water collecting under or beside the heater
  • Warped baseboards or stained drywall beside the water heater
  • A weeping drain spigot on the lower front of the heater

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Most leak mistakes happen before any tool comes out. Water travels along pipes and the outer jacket before it drips, so the puddle rarely marks the source, and harmless condensation on a gas unit or a cold tank can pass for a leak. Guessing leads to replacing the wrong valve or, worse, missing a tank that is on its way out. Safety matters here too: a relief valve that is discharging is protecting the tank, and plugging it is dangerous. Brittle old fittings can snap under force, and water around a 240-volt electric heater is a real shock hazard.

We work through leaks step by step and fix the cause along with the drip. We dry the unit, watch for fresh water, put a gauge on the system whenever the relief valve is involved, and check for thermal expansion problems that make valves weep. We replace valves and fittings with quality parts, swap plastic drain valves for brass, and add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve when pressure is the root problem. When the tank is the problem, we say so directly and explain replacement options, including the drain pan and leak protection that can keep the next failure from turning into water damage.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • A battery leak alarm beside the heater costs little and can catch a problem days sooner
  • A properly drained pan under the tank catches small leaks before they reach your floors
  • Replace a depleted anode rod before the steel tank becomes the next target for corrosion
  • An expansion tank that is present and properly charged keeps the relief valve from opening every cycle
  • A quick monthly look at the base, valves, and top connections catches leaks early
  • Check your water pressure; if it runs above about 80 psi, a pressure-reducing valve protects the tank and valves
  • Draining sediment every year helps the bottom of the tank last longer
