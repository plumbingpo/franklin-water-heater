The inside of a neglected water heater tells the whole story. On the bottom there is a bed of mineral sediment, sometimes several inches deep, that makes the burner work harder, causes that familiar rumbling, and can burn out the lower element on an electric unit. Hanging from the top is the anode rod, meant to corrode first, which may be down to bare wire after a few years. When it is used up, corrosion goes after the tank lining, and a leak is only a matter of time. For homeowners in Franklin, TN, a yearly visit is the simplest way to keep both of those problems from shortening the life of the heater.

We work through a full checklist every time. The tank is drained and flushed to clear the sediment layer. The anode rod comes out for inspection and is swapped when little of it remains. The T&P relief valve is tested so we know it will open under pressure and close again cleanly. On gas models, we check the burner, pilot assembly, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue, and draft. Electric heaters get element and thermostat testing and a check of the wiring connections. We also measure the air charge in the expansion tank, check the water pressure, set the thermostat to a scald-safe level unless a mixing valve allows higher storage, and inspect the drain valve, connections, and pan.

We maintain tankless units as well. In place of a sediment flush, they need periodic descaling, since hard water leaves mineral deposits in the heat exchanger that cut flow and efficiency. We isolate the unit, circulate a descaling solution through it, clean the inlet filter, check the vent and condensate line, and review stored error codes for patterns. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • Brown or orange tint in the hot water only
  • Temperature swings or reduced flow from a tankless heater
  • A tank that pops and knocks during a heating cycle
  • Hot water with a sulfur odor
  • Moisture, drips, or rust around the base or fittings
  • No memory of the last time the water heater was serviced
  • Hot water running out sooner than it used to

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Homeowners can handle the basics: keep the area clear, watch for drips, and pay attention to new sounds. The rest is harder than online videos make it seem. Flushing involves scalding water and an old plastic drain valve that may crack or refuse to seal. Anode rods are often seized in place and need significant force, sometimes with a flexible rod for low-clearance spaces. How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Flush annually, more often where the water is hard
  • Keep at least a couple of feet clear around the heater for air and service access
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years and replaced when it is mostly gone
  • Avoid turning the tank up past 120 degrees unless a mixing valve is installed
  • Ask about water treatment if flushing turns up heavy mineral buildup
  • Stay on top of tankless descaling to avoid error codes and reduced flow
  • Glance at the pan and connections regularly so small leaks are caught early
